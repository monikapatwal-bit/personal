An auction exists to discover a price. Yet the two tools our buyers had — SAP Ariba and our own first version — both forced the buyer to type that price in, then made them fight the interface to run the event. I redesigned the auction from that contradiction outward: buyers set the bounds, suppliers set the price, and the whole event finally lived in one place.

01 — Context

Auctions were the wedge — and we had two things to beat.

Aerchain is an AI-native enterprise procurement platform. For a large enterprise client, auctions were the wedge into competitive sourcing — reverse auctions to buy inputs, forward auctions to sell scrap.

But I wasn't designing in a vacuum. The redesign had to win on experience against SAP Ariba — the incumbent the client was moving off — and improve on our own first version of the auction module. I owned the research, the design, and the auction PRD; engineering built it.

❝

Two benchmarks, one bar: beat the tool buyers were leaving, and beat the one we shipped first.

02 — The Problem

Both tools fought the buyer — in the same place.

SAP Ariba was powerful but hostile. Buyers followed a live event through rank-only numbers on a dashboard that lagged around ten seconds, then clarified bids with suppliers over calls and WhatsApp. Suppliers were served so poorly that buyers routinely bid on their behalf — Ariba's own "Surrogate Bidder" workaround.

Our first version had the opposite failure: it was rigid. One format (English reverse), no lot structure, and a status vocabulary that showed the same auction as six different words across the app.

But both shared one flaw that gave the redesign its spine: on the create screen, each forced the buyer to enter a price — the exact number an auction exists to discover.

❝

The most confusing field in both tools was the price field — in a product whose entire job is to find the price.

03 — The Insight

An auction discovers the price. So stop asking the buyer for it.

The reframe was small, and it changed everything. A buyer shouldn't submit the price — they're accountable for the bounds. So buyers now set a ceiling (the most they'll pay) and a floor; suppliers provide the price by bidding between them.

One decision removed the most confusing input in both Ariba and our v1, and made the buyer's real job — defining the guardrails — explicit instead of implied.

❝

Design isn't always adding the right field. Sometimes it's deleting the wrong one.

04 — Design Decisions

Four opinionated calls.

1 · One guided flow for a family of formats — not five flows, not a config maze. Buyers pick from plain-language format cards (English Reverse / Forward, Dutch, Japanese), each explaining when to use it. Rejected: Ariba's raw configuration screen, and the alternative of five bespoke flows — both make buyers relearn the tool every time.

2 · Make broken setups impossible. Not every auction type works with every award format — a Dutch "first-to-accept" auction can't run item-level Cherry awards. I mapped the whole space into a compatibility matrix, and the product simply won't let a buyer build a structurally broken auction.

3 · Buyers set bounds; suppliers set price. The ceiling / floor model, made concrete in the create flow and the live event. Extra charges are labelled "supplier will provide," so the buyer is never asked for a number that isn't theirs.

4 · Bring the whole event in-platform. Real-time rank and bid history, plus 1:1 and broadcast negotiation inside the tool — so buyers stop clarifying over WhatsApp (Ariba) and stop flying blind (our v1). Auto-extend ends the "bid landed at the buzzer" disputes by design.

❝

The best guardrail isn't a warning message — it's a product that can't be set up wrong.

07 — Reflection

What stepping up taught me.

The biggest thing I learned is that domain depth is the prerequisite for influence. I could write the PRD, run the KTs, and make the architecture calls because I understood the problem space — not because I had a PM title. The title came later. The credibility came first.

Being the designer and the PM at once was a real advantage on a small team and a tight timeline: no translation layer between what the user needed and what got specced. What I saw in research went into the PRD; what I wrote in the PRD, I designed the next day.

The hardest part wasn't the workload — it was making decisions with incomplete information and not second-guessing them in public. That's the PM skill nobody talks about.
