01 — Context

One format, on a platform that needed a family of them.

Auctions let a buyer put line items in front of invited suppliers and let competition set the price. Aerchain's module supported exactly one mechanism — an English reverse auction (price starts high, suppliers bid it down). It was live with a real enterprise buyer running genuine sourcing events, across a buyer app and a separate supplier app.

My role was research and product design: I ran the audit and competitive research, defined the redesign, and prototyped it end to end. Implementation was owned by the engineering team — I don't claim the code as mine.

02 — The Problem

Three things the product got wrong.

1 · Only one auction format worked. English reverse was the sole supported mechanism. Buyers who needed a Dutch clock, a Japanese last-standing round, or a forward auction couldn't run one.

2 · Buyers were forced to enter the price the auction should discover. On the create form, Price and Amount were mandatory for the buyer — even though the whole point of an auction is for suppliers to submit price. Buyers were blocked on a field that wasn't theirs.

3 · The status vocabulary was a maze. The same auction appeared as "Released", "Processed", "Pre-Bid Submitted", "converted", "live", "pending" — across a listing, a lifecycle rail, and a separate supplier-response tab set. No single, followable state.

03 — Research

How mature platforms model a family of formats.

Before redesigning, I studied how mature sourcing platforms model auctions — most closely SAP Ariba, the enterprise reference point. The pattern is consistent: buyers expect a family of formats across reverse and forward directions, each suited to a different situation.

  • English reverse — price starts high, suppliers bid down; full transparency (rank, lead bid, gap).
  • Dutch — price rises at set intervals from a low start; first to accept wins. Fast, decisive.
  • Japanese (clock) — price moves in fixed rounds; suppliers must actively accept to stay in; last standing wins.
  • Forward — the same formats, inverted, for selling rather than buying.

Sources: SAP Help — reverse auctions; SAP Learning — auction formats.

04 — Design

Three decisions that carried the redesign.

1 · One guided wizard for all five formats — not five separate flows. Auction type is a set of plain-language cards (English Forward / English Reverse / Dutch / Japanese Clock), each explaining what it does and when to use it, with English Reverse recommended by default. Format-specific rules appear only when relevant. Rejected: a picker branching into five bespoke flows — multiplies maintenance and forces buyers to relearn the tool per format. One wizard keeps a single mental model; the trade-off is heavier conditional logic.

2 · Buyers set bounds; suppliers provide the price. Instead of a mandatory Price/Amount, buyers set a Ceiling and Floor — the guardrails the auction runs between — and additional charges are labelled "supplier will provide amounts." Rejected: keeping one price field and just making it optional — leaves the concept muddled. Ceiling/floor makes the buyer's real job (setting bounds) explicit.

3 · One lifecycle vocabulary: Draft → Pre-Live → Live → Post-Live → Cancelled. The tangle of Released / Processed / Pre-Bid / converted collapses into one ordered lifecycle used consistently across listing, detail, and supplier views. Rejected: renaming statuses in place without unifying them — cosmetic, and the same auction would still read differently on different screens.

05 — Before & After

One five-step wizard: Details → Items → Suppliers → Timing → Review.

The redesigned create flow is a five-step wizard — Details → Items → Suppliers → Timing & Rules → Review — with the format cards, ceiling/floor items, AI-ranked supplier recommendations, and a review-and-publish summary.

07 — Outcome & Honesty

A validated prototype — and honest about it.

The redesign is a validated prototype, not yet shipped to production. So I'm not attaching usage numbers to it — the auctions running live today are the old experience I'm replacing, not a result of this work.

What I can stand behind is verifiable in the interface: formats went from one to five, the create flow is a single guided wizard, the price model no longer blocks buyers on a field that isn't theirs, and the status vocabulary is one ordered lifecycle instead of six overlapping labels.

How I'd measure it once it ships:

  • Format mix — which of the five formats buyers actually choose.
  • Create-to-publish completion and time-to-setup, vs the old flow.
  • Supplier participation funnel — invited → viewed → bid (already surfaced per auction).
  • Realised savings vs ceiling, by format.
