How it started

When our PM moved off the project halfway through, I wasn't handed a design brief. I was handed an unfinished product, a three-month window, and an enterprise client whose core requirement our platform couldn't meet.

I'd sat in every client call for months as the designer, so I already knew the gaps better than any document did. Over the next three months I studied five competing platforms, wrote the product spec from scratch, ran handover sessions with our CTO, wrote every user story — and designed every screen I specced.

The first thing I did was open our own auction tool and try to answer one question: what actually happens during an auction? It took about a day to find the honest answer.

02 — What an auction is

What an auction actually is.

Most large-company buying happens over email. A buyer sends a request to a handful of suppliers, collects quotes back over days or weeks, and picks one.

An auction compresses all of that into a single timed event. The buyer invites suppliers into a scheduled window — often under half an hour — where they bid against each other in real time. Because the buyer is purchasing rather than selling, the price moves down instead of up. The lowest bid leads.

Done well, it turns weeks of back-and-forth into one afternoon and a defensible record. Done badly, suppliers don't show up, half the items get no bids, and the buyer finds out when it's already too late to fix.

The auction lifecycle
1
Set up
Buyer builds the event — items, starting prices, bidding rules, and which suppliers to invite.
days
2
Invite & pre-bid
Suppliers accept the invitation and submit an opening price before the live event opens.
days
3
Live bidding
Everyone bids at once against a countdown. Each new lowest bid changes the ranking, live.
~24 min
4
Close & award
Bidding stops, the result locks, and the buyer awards the business.
minutes

Days of preparation for roughly twenty-four minutes that decide the price.

Five words used later on this page
Lot
A group of items bid on and awarded together. Splitting a hundred items into ten lots lets different suppliers win different parts.
Pre-bid
An opening price submitted before the live event. It sets the starting point everyone bids down from.
L1
The leading bid — the lowest price currently on the table. L2 is second, and so on.
Ceiling price
The most the buyer will pay. Bids above it are blocked, so it also doubles as the baseline savings are measured against.
Extension
Extra time added when someone bids in the last few minutes, so a supplier can't win by hiding until the clock runs out.

03 — The problem

We hadn't built an auction. We'd built a quote form with a clock on it.

The problem wasn't a missing feature list. It was that the auction had been built as a variation on our quotation tool — the thing that collects static prices over email — rather than as a live event.

The product said so out loud. On the supplier's own invitation screen, the event type field read RFQ, with the auction listed underneath as a property of it.

Image placeholder · key screenshotv4 supplier invitation — Event Type reads RFQ, with "auction" underneath as a property. Annotate the RFQ value; redact any client branding.

Once you see that, everything else follows.

The event had no shape

The status timeline on every buyer screen was a purchase pipeline: New, Creation Date, Auction Release, then approval, purchase order, receipt. The entire live auction — the thing the product exists for — was one dot on it. No pre-bid stage. No live stage. No completed stage.

Image placeholderv4 buyer detail — the lifecycle timeline, a purchase pipeline with the auction as one dot.

Participation was counted in emails

The participation card showed Sent, Delivered, Viewed, Bids, Rejected. Those are mailing metrics. They can tell a buyer that three invitations were delivered. They cannot tell a buyer that a supplier accepted and then went quiet — which is the only question worth asking in the hour before an auction opens.

Image placeholderv4 Supplier Participation card — Sent, Delivered, Viewed, Bids, Rejected.

Extending a live auction was a row of buttons

Plus five minutes, plus ten, plus fifteen, plus twenty, plus twenty-five, plus thirty. No reason field. No record of why. An action with contractual consequences, given the interaction weight of a microwave.

Image placeholderv4 buyer live view — empty trend chart, zeroed insights, and a row of extension buttons.

And when it ended, it just ended

The supplier who had spent the last half hour competing got a white card, a blue checkmark, and a timestamp. No rank. No result. No indication of whether they'd won.

Image placeholderv4 supplier "Auction has been ended" modal — keep the white space; the emptiness is the point.

What surprised me

Our post-auction analysis was genuinely good. Item-by-supplier comparison grids, five award scenarios, bid version history logged down to the IP address, initial-versus-final analysis per item and per supplier. All of it inherited from the quotation module.

Image placeholderv4 Analysis tab — mature evaluation capability, inherited wholesale, and the reason nobody noticed the event layer was missing.

We were sophisticated at judging bids and primitive at running the event that produced them. That was the whole problem, and it had been invisible because the half we were good at was the half people looked at afterwards.

An auction isn't a document. It's an event. We had built a document.

04 — Market research

Before deciding what to build, I wanted to know what good already looked like.

I had three months and no spec to work from. Rather than start designing, I spent the first stretch studying what a serious auction module already contains — working from event rule guides, supplier manuals and filed patents rather than marketing pages.

Sources

Compared across

Auction formats, lot scheduling, bid rules, the live event screen, and audit and export.

Also looked at

Terminology and defaults, so a buyer arriving from Ariba would recognise our screens.

Three things came out of it.

Three key findings

  1. These tools help you pick a winner. They don't help you run the event.

    Every big platform puts its effort into comparing bids after the auction closes. Almost nothing goes into the hour when suppliers are actually bidding.

    Keelvar, Coupa and Jaggaer all lead with award analysis. None leads with the live screen.

  2. SAP hit a hard problem, patented the fix, then switched the feature off.

    When lots close one after another, a last-second bid can push one lot's end time into the next lot's slot. SAP holds a patent describing how to handle this. In their shipping product they simply disable extensions in that mode.

    The problem was real, solvable, and nobody had claimed it.

  3. Nobody checks whether an auction will work before it starts.

    If half your items have no supplier interest, you find out when bidding opens. By then it's too late. The industry's answer is a reminder email the day before.

    Ariba's own guidance treats supplier coverage as a question for the buyer to think about, not something the product answers.

05 — What I chose to build

Three months buys you one bet. I made it on the event.

I stopped trying to win on evaluation. Keelvar's entire company is award optimisation — modelling scenarios against supplier caps, regional continuity, capacity limits. Ariba and Jaggaer both ship optimisation across millions of variables. We were not closing that gap in a quarter, and a shallow version of something three companies already do well would have been worse than nothing.

What nobody was doing well was the event itself. So that's where the work went.

Decision 1

Compatibility as a product decision, not an engineering one

I built a matrix of auction type against award format and documented why each incompatible pair fails. Dutch instant-accept can't support item-level comparison. Japanese clock elimination conflicts with independent item allocation.

Deciding what we wouldn't build, with the reasoning attached, is what protected the timeline. Nobody had to relitigate it mid-sprint.

Decision 2

Pre-bid as the leverage point

The obvious investment was the live bidding screen. The high-leverage one was the two hours before it. The live event is only ever as good as the data that walks into it.

So the pre-bid phase got a structured wizard, item-level coverage scoring, and a panel that names weak items and silent suppliers while there's still time to act on either.

Decision 3

A ceiling price per supplier

The original logic used one buyer-defined ceiling for everyone. I changed it so each supplier's own pre-bid becomes their reference point in the live event.

That turns a static gate into a per-supplier baseline, and makes savings measurable per relationship rather than only in aggregate.

Decision 4

One baseline for savings

When there's no pre-bid phase, ceiling price becomes the benchmark — in every format, every time.

That sounds like a spec detail. It isn't. Savings methodology in reverse auctions is genuinely contested, and published industry benchmarks swing from 19% to 7% across consecutive quarters partly because everyone measures differently. Fixing one baseline is what makes our numbers comparable event to event.

And what I deliberately left out

Award optimisation. Package bids with contingent discounts. Multi-currency. Bid transformation for landed cost. Each of these is real capability that competitors ship and we don't. Each would have consumed the quarter on its own. Naming them as out of scope in the spec — rather than leaving them ambiguous — is what let the team move without stopping to argue about them.

06 — The pivotal moment

The change that looked like a schema migration.

Our pilot client ran procurement in three levels: programme, then category, then line item. Our spec structured auction data differently. On the surface this was a schema change — weeks of backend work and a timeline slip we had no room for.

I spent a day mapping their hierarchy against what we'd already built, and found it fitted exactly. Not as a workaround — as a configuration convention. Their programme was an auction. Their categories were lots. Their line items were items.

The best product decisions look obvious in hindsight. That's how you know they were right.

07 — What we built

Six things, in the order they happen.

Setup

Four formats, one flow

English forward and reverse, Dutch, and Japanese clock auctions — each with different bidding logic, all created through the same five-step wizard. The compatibility matrix sits behind it, so the system won't let a buyer configure a combination that can't work.

Image placeholderCreate wizard, step 1 — format selection.

Lots that run on their own clocks

Group items into named lots and choose how they open and close: all together, one after another, or closing at staggered intervals. A hundred-lot event stops being a hundred separate auctions.

And when a late bid extends one lot in sequential mode, only that lot extends — every lot after it shifts forward to absorb the change. The market leader disables extensions entirely rather than solve this. A buyer running thirty sequential lots shouldn't have to choose between anti-sniping protection and a schedule.

Image placeholderScheduling mode cards + serial lot timing table.

Before the auction

Needs Attention

Before the auction opens, the platform names the items nobody has bid on and the suppliers who accepted the invite and then went quiet — with a nudge button beside each one.

The industry's answer to this is a reminder email the day before. Ours is a list of exactly what's wrong and who to chase, while there's still time to fix it.

Image placeholderInvited status → Overview tab → Needs Attention panel.

During

A live event you can read

Every bid updates the screen in real time — rank, savings, and which items are hot, active or going cold based on how many actively bidding suppliers have touched them.

Suppliers show as active, idle or inactive on interaction thresholds, so a buyer knows who's gone quiet while there's still time to do something about it. In a twenty-four minute event, that matters.

Image placeholderBuyer Live view, Items tab.

During · supplier side

Smart Bids

On the supplier side, the platform works out the exact minimum bid needed to take the lead on every item they're losing, and submits them together. Competitive without overshooting — because a supplier who feels the process was fair is a supplier who turns up to your next event.

Image placeholderSupplier live view with Smart Bids.

After

A record you can defend

Three reports generate at close: full bid progression, a tamper-evident audit trail with every actor, IP address and timestamp under a document reference, and a complete log of every message exchanged.

Every disruptive action during the event — a pause, an extension, a disqualification — carries the reason its buyer gave into that record. What used to be an unanswerable question is now a download.

09 — What I'd still build

The gaps I'd rather name than imply aren't there.

Three months bought us an event system. It didn't buy us everything, and I'd rather name the gaps than imply they aren't there.

Award optimisation

We ship five preset award scenarios. Keelvar models award structures against real constraints — supplier caps, regional continuity, capacity limits. That's a different class of capability and we don't have it.

Combinatorial bidding

A supplier who could deliver lots three, seven and nine more cheaply as a bundle currently has nowhere to say so. Package bids with contingent discounts are the clearest next unlock.

Multi-currency

Deferred to the next release, and it's the single thing that limits how global this can go. A cross-border supplier can't bid in their own currency and have the ranking normalise automatically.

Supplier-facing exports

Suppliers can't yet download their own record of an event they took part in. Given how much of this rebuild was justified by making the process feel fair, that's the gap that bothers me most.

What three months taught me

Domain depth is the fastest onboarding there is

I picked up product management responsibilities in days rather than weeks, entirely because I'd been in every client call as the designer. Proximity to the customer compounds in ways an org chart doesn't capture.

Knowing the market changes what you build, not just how you pitch it

Studying five platforms didn't give me a feature list to copy. It told me which gaps were expected-and-missing, which were genuinely open, and — most usefully — which fights not to pick.

The pre-bid phase had more leverage than the live auction

The live event is the part everyone wants to design. It is only ever as good as the data that walks into it.

Deferred decisions don't disappear

The one place I let ambiguity sit — conditional cherry-picking logic for lots — came back during build, with less time and more people in the room. It cost more then than deciding it in week two would have.

Specifying and designing the same screen makes both better

Every requirement I wrote, I had to draw. Anything I couldn't draw wasn't actually specified yet.

The auction still isn't finished. It's just finally an auction.
