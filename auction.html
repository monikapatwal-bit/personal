When our PM transitioned off mid-project, I wasn't handed a design brief — I was handed an unfinished product. Aerchain's auction module supported exactly one format, and the enterprise client we were chasing needed something it couldn't do. 01 — What an auction is

Live price discovery, not a form.

The platform

Aerchain is an enterprise procurement platform — the system large organisations use to run sourcing events, negotiate with suppliers, and award contracts. It competes with SAP Ariba, Coupa, Jaggaer, and Keelvar, and was already live with one of India's largest conglomerates on the RFX module.

The auction

An auction is how a buyer runs live competitive price discovery. Instead of collecting static quotes over email, the buyer invites suppliers into a timed event where they bid against each other in real time — and the platform captures every bid, every rank change, and every rupee of savings as it happens.

Aerchain had a version of this. It could run exactly one kind: a List auction, where one supplier wins every item. That works for single-vendor contracts. It does not work for a procurement team splitting a category across regions, running a hundred lots on independent timers, or needing a defensible record afterwards.

02 — The problem

It could run an auction. It couldn't run procurement.

The existing module treated an auction as a single event with a single winner. Real enterprise procurement isn't shaped like that — it's bundled, scheduled, audited, and negotiated. Sitting in the client calls as the designer, I watched the same four gaps surface in every conversation.

  • The format ceilingOne format meant one shape of deal. Enterprise buyers wanted items grouped into lots with independent timers — and we had to say no in the room.
  • The savings paradoxThe whole point of an auction is savings. Ours were calculated manually, in a spreadsheet, after the event closed. The number that justified the tool arrived too late to act on.
  • The pre-bid blind spotBuyers discovered half their items had no supplier coverage after the auction went live. By then there was nothing to do. The live event was only ever as good as what walked into it.
  • The compliance voidNo audit trail. For regulated procurement teams, an event they can't defend line-by-line to an auditor is an event they can't run.

The top half is a twenty-year-old standard we were behind on. The bottom half is whitespace nobody had reached — which is where the redesign went.

Three findings

  1. These tools help you pick a winner. They don't help you run the event.Every major platform puts its effort into comparing bids after the auction closes. Almost nothing goes into the hour when suppliers are actually bidding — Keelvar, Coupa and Jaggaer all lead with award analysis; none leads with the live screen.
  2. SAP hit a hard problem, patented the fix, then switched it off.Ariba disables overtime entirely in serial-lot bidding — while holding a patent (US 7152043) that describes how to resolve the underlying scheduling collision. The problem was real, solvable, and unclaimed.
  3. Nobody checks whether an auction will work before it starts.The industry's answer to weak supplier coverage is a reminder email the day before. Ariba's own guidance treats coverage as a question for the buyer to think about, not something the product answers.

What the research settled

  • Savings has no agreed benchmark.Keelvar claims 3–25%, Zycus 5–20%, Jaggaer 10–15% (relative), and Unit4's measured quarters run 7.6–19.6%. Fixing ceiling price as the savings baseline is a stance on a contested question — not a spec detail.
  • Indian buyers are calibrated on fixed rules.GeM extends by a fixed 15 minutes on any late bid, every time. Every Indian procurement pro carries that mental model — so a configurable extension is more powerful but less familiar. An adoption consideration, not just a feature.
  • Audit is commercial, not compliance.The EU has permitted e-auctions for two decades, yet they're under 1% of public contracts — the gap is trust and defensibility. That's what makes a tamper-evident record worth building.
  • The supplier side is substantive, not cosmetic.The research on reverse auctions finds a supplier's perception of procedural fairness drives the relationship — so readable rules, visible bid history and a stated reason on any pause are design decisions, not decoration.

Sources: each platform's own product documentation and learning material; SAP patent US 7152043; EU Directive 2004/18/EC and European Parliament usage figures; academic work on reverse-auction savings methodology and buyer–supplier relationships. Compiled July 2026. "Not found" in the matrix means absent from public docs, which is weaker than proven absence.

04 — What I chose to build

Close the twenty-year gap — and pick what to close first.

Most of what v4 lacked was already standard: parallel and serial lots, Dutch and Japanese formats, decrement rules, pre-bid phases, audit logs. The job wasn't inventing a category — it was closing a twenty-year gap in three months, and deciding what to close first. Three decisions did the heavy lifting.

  • Compatibility as a product decision, not an engineering one.I built an auction-type × award-format matrix and documented why each incompatible pair fails — Dutch instant-accept can't support item-level comparative evaluation; Japanese clock elimination conflicts with independent item allocation. Deciding what we wouldn't build, with reasoning, protected the three-month window.
  • Pre-bid as the leverage point.The market's answer to weak coverage was a reminder email. I specced a pre-bid wizard, item-level coverage scoring, and a Needs Attention panel that surfaces weak items and silent suppliers while there's still time to fix them — the one place the whole category had left empty.
  • A dynamic ceiling per supplier.The original logic used a single buyer-defined ceiling. I changed it so each supplier's own pre-bid becomes their ceiling reference in the live event — turning a static gate into a per-supplier baseline, and making savings measurable per relationship rather than in aggregate.

05 — The pivotal moment

The change that looked like a schema migration.

Our enterprise pilot client ran procurement in three levels: programme → category → line item. The PRD structured auction data differently. On the surface this was a schema change — weeks of backend work and a timeline slip we didn't have room for.

I spent a day mapping their hierarchy against what we'd already built and found it fit exactly onto Auction → Lot → Item. Not as a workaround — as a configuration convention. Their programme was an auction, their categories were lots, their line items were items.

Zero engineering cost. Zero timeline impact. And it turned out to be the correct long-term data model for every lot-based auction we've built since. Sign-off came the same week.

The best product decisions look obvious in hindsight. That's how you know they were right.

06 — Key features

From one auction format to a procurement event system.

  • Four Auction Formats, One FlowEnglish Forward and Reverse, Dutch, and Japanese clock auctions — each with its own bidding logic, all created through the same five-step wizard. Buyers pick the negotiation style that fits the category, not the one the software supports.
  • Lots That Run on Their Own ClocksGroup items into named lots and choose how they open and close: all at once, one after another, or at staggered intervals. A hundred-lot event no longer means a hundred separate auctions.
  • Needs AttentionBefore the auction goes live, the platform flags which items nobody has bid on and which suppliers accepted the invite but went quiet — with a one-click nudge on the same screen. The gaps get found while they're still fixable.
  • A Live Event You Can Actually ReadEvery bid updates the screen in real time — rank changes, savings percentage, and which items are hot, active, or going cold. Suppliers show as active, idle, or inactive, so the buyer knows who to chase mid-event.
  • Smart BidsOn the supplier side, the platform calculates the exact minimum bid needed to take the lead on every item that needs one — and lets them submit all of it at once. Competitive without overshooting.
  • Buyer-Approved Bid ResetA supplier who fat-fingers a bid can request a correction; the buyer approves it and the reset is logged. A safety valve no competitor documented.
  • A Record You Can DefendThree reports at close: full bid progression, a tamper-evident audit trail with every actor and timestamp, and a complete message log. What used to be an unanswerable question is now a download.

08 — Gaps & reflection

What three months as an accidental PM taught me.

  • Domain depth is the fastest onboarding there is.I picked up PM responsibilities in days, not weeks — entirely because I'd been in every client call as the designer. Proximity to the customer compounds in ways an org chart doesn't capture.
  • The pre-bid phase had more leverage than the live auction.The live event is the part everyone wants to design. It's only as good as the data that walks into it.
  • Deferred decisions don't disappear.The one place I let ambiguity sit — conditional cherry-picking logic for lots — came back during build, with less time and more people in the room. It cost more then than it would have in week two.
  • Specifying and designing the same screen makes both better.Every requirement I wrote, I had to draw. Anything that couldn't be drawn wasn't actually specified yet.

Honest limits of the work

The research had no primary supplier interviews — everything supplier-side came from vendor documentation and academic work. Five conversations with suppliers who bid regularly would be the single highest-value addition, and genuinely original: nobody publishes it. And the module shipped the standards first; several of the "where nobody was" bets are live with the pilot but not yet measured against a baseline.
