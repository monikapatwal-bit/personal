When our PM transitioned off mid-project, I wasn't handed a design brief — I was handed an unfinished product. Aerchain's auction module supported exactly one format, and the enterprise client we were chasing needed something it couldn't do. There was no spec, no user stories, and a build window of three months.

I'd sat in every client call as the designer. That turned out to be the fastest onboarding I could have asked for. Over the next three months I benchmarked five competing platforms, wrote the PRD from scratch, ran KT sessions with our CTO, wrote every user story — and designed every screen I specced. What started as a gap in our product roadmap became the module that took us live with a global enterprise client.

01 — Understanding the Module & the Problem

What the module is, and why it needed a rebuild.

What is Aerchain?

Aerchain is an enterprise procurement platform — the system large organisations use to run sourcing events, negotiate with suppliers, and award contracts. It competes with SAP Ariba, Coupa, Jaggaer, and Keelvar, and was already live with one of India's largest conglomerates on the RFX module.

What is the Auction Module?

An auction is how a buyer runs live competitive price discovery. Instead of collecting static quotes over email, the buyer invites suppliers into a timed event where they bid against each other in real time — and the platform captures every bid, every rank change, and every rupee of savings as it happens.

Aerchain had a version of this. It could run exactly one kind of auction: a List auction, where one supplier wins every item. That works for single-vendor contracts. It does not work for a procurement team splitting a category across regions, running a hundred lots on independent timers, or needing a defensible record of the whole event afterwards.

The auction we had could run an auction. It couldn't run procurement.

The existing module treated an auction as a single event with a single winner. Real enterprise procurement isn't shaped like that — it's bundled, scheduled, audited, and negotiated. Every deal conversation surfaced the same four gaps.

  • The format ceilingOne format meant one shape of deal. Enterprise buyers wanted items grouped into lots with independent timers — and we had to say no in the room.
  • The savings paradoxThe whole point of an auction is savings. Ours were calculated manually, in a spreadsheet, after the event had closed. The number that justified the tool arrived too late to act on.
  • The pre-bid blind spotBuyers discovered half their items had no supplier coverage after the auction went live. By then there was nothing to do. The live event was only ever as good as what walked into it.
  • The compliance voidNo audit trail. For regulated procurement teams, an event they can't defend line-by-line to an auditor is an event they can't run.

02 — My Approach

Learn the domain first. Spec against it second.

Phase 1 — Learning the domain before writing a line of spec

Competitive benchmarking · 5 platforms

I studied SAP Ariba, Keelvar, Coupa, Jaggaer, and Prokuria — not just their features, but their terminology, their defaults, and the buyer mental models baked into their flows. In enterprise procurement, naming is usability: a buyer coming from Ariba should recognise what they're looking at.

  1. Lot scheduling was table stakes — every serious platform had it, we had none.
  2. Staggered Closing is the industry-recommended pattern for high-item-count events.
  3. No competitor had a structured pre-bid commercial-terms phase — an open differentiation gap.
  4. Terminology diverged wildly; aligning to Ariba / Keelvar conventions reduced onboarding friction.
  5. Audit and export were treated as compliance checkboxes, not product surface — an opening.

Client-call archaeology · the designer's advantage

I'd been in every client call for months as the designer. Instead of running fresh discovery, I went back through those conversations looking for the moments buyers got frustrated and nobody wrote it down.

  1. Buyers were chasing suppliers over email to submit pre-bids — the single biggest time sink in the event.
  2. "Which items are actually getting bids?" had no answer until the auction was over.
  3. Suppliers dropped out silently; buyers had no way to see who had gone quiet.
  4. Savings were reconstructed after the fact and always contested.
  5. Every buyer asked some version of "can I get a record of this?"

Phase 2 — Strategic product decisions

  • Compatibility as a product decision, not an engineering one.I built an auction-type × award-format matrix and documented why each incompatible pair fails — Dutch instant-accept can't support item-level comparative evaluation; Japanese clock elimination conflicts with independent item allocation. Deciding what we wouldn't build, with reasoning, protected the three-month window.
  • Pre-bid as the leverage point.The obvious place to invest was the live bidding screen. The high-leverage place was the phase before it. I specced a pre-bid wizard, item-level coverage scoring, and a Needs Attention panel that surfaces weak items and silent suppliers while there's still time to fix them.
  • A dynamic ceiling per supplier.The original logic used a single buyer-defined ceiling. I changed it so each supplier's own pre-bid becomes their ceiling reference in the live event — turning a static gate into a per-supplier baseline, and making savings measurable per relationship rather than in aggregate.

03 — The Pivotal Moment

The change that looked like a schema migration.

Our enterprise pilot client ran procurement in three levels: programme → category → line item. The PRD structured auction data differently. On the surface this was a schema change — weeks of backend work and a timeline slip we didn't have room for.

I spent a day mapping their hierarchy against what we'd already built and found it fit exactly onto Auction → Lot → Item. Not as a workaround — as a configuration convention. Their programme was an auction, their categories were lots, their line items were items.

Zero engineering cost. Zero timeline impact. And it turned out to be the correct long-term data model for every lot-based auction we've built since. Sign-off came the same week.

The best product decisions look obvious in hindsight. That's how you know they were right.

04 — Key Features

From one auction format to a procurement event system.

  • Four Auction Formats, One FlowEnglish Forward and Reverse, Dutch, and Japanese clock auctions — each with its own bidding logic, all created through the same five-step wizard. Buyers pick the negotiation style that fits the category, not the one the software supports.
  • Lots That Run on Their Own ClocksGroup items into named lots and choose how they open and close: all at once, one after another, or at staggered intervals. A hundred-lot event no longer means a hundred separate auctions.
  • Needs AttentionBefore the auction goes live, the platform flags which items nobody has bid on and which suppliers accepted the invite but went quiet — with a one-click nudge on the same screen. The gaps get found while they're still fixable.
  • A Live Event You Can Actually ReadEvery bid updates the screen in real time — rank changes, savings percentage, and which items are hot, active, or going cold. Suppliers show as active, idle, or inactive, so the buyer knows who to chase mid-event.
  • Smart BidsOn the supplier side, the platform calculates the exact minimum bid needed to take the lead on every item that needs one — and lets them submit all of it at once. Competitive without overshooting.
  • A Record You Can DefendThree reports at close: full bid progression, a tamper-evident audit trail with every actor and timestamp, and a complete message log. What used to be an unanswerable question is now a download.

06 — Reflection

What three months as an accidental PM taught me.

  • Domain depth is the fastest onboarding there is.I picked up PM responsibilities in days, not weeks — entirely because I'd been in every client call as the designer. Proximity to the customer compounds in ways an org chart doesn't capture.
  • The pre-bid phase had more leverage than the live auction.The live event is the part everyone wants to design. It's only as good as the data that walks into it.
  • Deferred decisions don't disappear.The one place I let ambiguity sit — conditional cherry-picking logic for lots — came back during build, with less time and more people in the room. It cost more then than it would have in week two.
  • Specifying and designing the same screen makes both better.Every requirement I wrote, I had to draw. Anything that couldn't be drawn wasn't actually specified yet.
